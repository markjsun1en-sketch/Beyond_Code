# Chapter 11. Cognitive Overload
## When Changes That Need Judgment Exceed Qualified Review Capacity

Chapter 10 looked at how risk, impact, reversibility, and evidence determine the weight of a review. A Review Budget assumes the project has enough qualified capacity to handle important changes before a decision is due. That assumption can fail.

AI can expand the production of code, tests, documents, rules, and workflows. It can also increase search, analysis, and verification capacity. Project size, parallel work, historical state, governance, and external dependencies can grow at the same time. To understand overload, ask whether retained changes that need human judgment are persistently exceeding qualified review capacity within the relevant decision window. Then ask which response fits the cause.

Reducing work in progress, narrowing changes, adding review capacity, strengthening deterministic verification, and routing direct review by risk are different responses. A *Human Control Plane* (HCP), introduced in Chapter 12, is one optional way of organizing the decision interface. Authority comes from the applicable grants. *Human Judgment Load* (HJL) names a burden we can examine in parts. The v4.94 and SP122 episodes in this chapter provide local observations.

---

## 11.1 Cognitive Overload Is a Condition

*Cognitive Overload* is a condition in which engineering changes that need understanding and judgment exceed the team's ability to reach sufficiently reliable judgments within the available time and risk constraints. Three qualifications matter.

First, there must be real demand for judgment. Ten thousand discarded candidate files may cost storage and triage. A ten-line irreversible data migration may require intensive human judgment. Object count alone tells us little.

Second, the mismatch has to occur in the relevant decision window. Projects can pause new work, extend review, bring in a specialist, or narrow a release. Overload becomes a structural risk when changes keep arriving faster than the team can handle them, or a decision is due before capacity returns.

Third, understanding has to serve the decision. Teams already work without holding the internals of every compiler, dependency, and platform in mind. *Decision-sufficient Understanding* means understanding enough for the current Claim, risk, and responsibility.

```text
AI Output
    >
Human Capacity
```

That slogan hides both the work and the interval over which it must be judged. A more useful statement is conditional.

```text
Judgment-bearing retained change arrival
                    >
Available qualified review capacity
over the relevant decision window
```

Both sides can change. Any numerical comparison needs separately calibrated measures.

## 11.2 Five Quantities Behind the Word Throughput

| Quantity | Question | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Gross Output Throughput | How many candidates did the system generate? | Which were retained or need human judgment |
| Retained Change Throughput | How many changes entered the current worktree, baseline, or runtime? | Which are correct, necessary, or high-risk |
| Machine-checked Throughput | How many changes ran through named automated checks? | Whether checks cover all relevant Claims |
| Judgment-bearing Throughput | How many changes still need product, architecture, security, experience, or responsibility judgments? | How much effort and risk each judgment carries |
| Qualified Review Capacity | How much review matching the claims and risks can the team do in this window? | How much that capacity can change |

Generation can increase both raw output and machine-checked throughput. Tools, modularity, impact analysis, test generation, and summaries can also increase review capacity. Large changes, shared state, excessive governance, document debt, and unclear authority can reduce it.

After selection, verification, and routing by risk, ask what kind of human judgment remains for each retained change and whether the team can decide before the judgment becomes stale.

## 11.3 Review Has a Purpose Beyond Reading

Chapter 10 defines Review, Review Target, Finding, Review Budget, Review Independence, and Review Disposition. Here the concern is their demand on qualified human capacity.

A review can require several steps.

1. Confirm the target and the decision it serves.
2. Understand intent, scope, and non-goals.
3. Identify the actual change.
4. Inspect evidence that matches the Claim.
5. Judge risks, alternatives, and unknowns.
6. Confirm authority and intended use.
7. Record findings and a recommendation. A reviewer with an applicable Authority Grant can also issue a Review Disposition through a Decision Record.
8. Keep a way to inspect sources, take over, and recover.

### Local, Reversible, Low-Risk Changes

A small diff, deterministic checks, focused tests, automated impact analysis, sampling, and an explicit rollback can often be enough.

### High-Risk Changes or Changes That Depend on Subjective Judgment

The task can require a line-by-line review, a security boundary inspection, a data migration rehearsal, architectural judgment, a product experience review, a creative or aesthetic decision, or independent specialist review.

Use the Claim, risk, reversibility, evidence coverage, and human authority to choose how much of the implementation to inspect directly. In a complex system, someone needs to reconstruct a local model sufficient for the decision and know where to go deeper.

## 11.4 Five Ways to Diagnose Overload

The following perspectives overlap. They help locate the burden in a particular project. Their completeness and how they generalize across projects remain open.

### 11.4.1 Output Overload

Too many objects reach the production or review surface.

```text
Code
Tests
Rules
Documents
Plans
Reports
Evidence
Logs
Assets
Configuration
```

A review queue may keep growing. Reports and documents may repeat one another. Reviewers may read summaries and skip the sources. Low-value changes compete for attention with high-risk changes. Many candidates are neither adopted nor explicitly retired. Start by filtering, removing duplicates, limiting arrival rates, and narrowing scope.

### 11.4.2 State Overload

The team struggles to tell which rules apply, which workflow is running, which baseline was accepted, which assumptions remain open, which capabilities have which authority, which evidence has expired, and whether the worktree, runtime, and released state match.

State can be copied across several surfaces. Versions and responsibilities can be unclear. History can be mixed in with what applies now. Another summary can help someone find a record or become a competing source. Chapter 6's distinctions among source, version, scope, freshness, and conflict become important here.

### 11.4.3 Dependency Overload

Understanding one local change can require following effects through many systems.

```text
Intent / Policy
       ↓
Workflow
       ↓
Agent / Skill / Tool
       ↓
Product Change
       ↓
Runtime / User Experience
       ↓
Evidence / Decision
```

Dependencies can be implicit, changing, and spread across repositories. Important effects may go unnoticed. The reviewer may have no way to identify the next system to check. A local success can be mistaken for an end-to-end one. Intent and authority can be lost along a chain. Recovery can address a visible result while leaving downstream state damaged.

Smaller components, clear contracts, impact analysis, and traceable references can reduce this work. A single manifest that copies every dependency still needs to be understood and maintained.

### 11.4.4 Judgment Overload

The scarce capacity can be high-quality judgment.

```text
Is this change necessary?
Is the scope right for the goal?
Which decision can this evidence support?
Does the security boundary need to be tighter?
Does the experience trade-off fit the product's direction?
Should we keep iterating, roll back, or stop?
```

Judgment Overload occurs when many changes cannot be handled with deterministic rules alone. Training, tools, division of work, experts, and better information can expand capacity. Decisions about value, accountability, risk acceptance, and creative direction stay relevant even when generating candidates costs less.

### 11.4.5 Meta-level Overload

The team must also judge the system that produces the software.

- Is the Agent Contract appropriate?
- Does the Workflow fit the current phase?
- Does the gate address a real risk?
- Did the validator observe the right object?
- Did memory amplify a stale conclusion?
- Is governance adding things to prove without adding evidence about delivery?
- Is self-evolution turning a local success into a general rule?

*Governance Overload* fits this perspective. Gates, reviews, manifests, and evidence requests create state, dependencies, and new decisions. Include their cost in the Cognitive Load Budget.

## 11.5 Several Causes Can Produce the Same Mismatch

| Possible source | How it adds load | Alternative explanation or check |
|---|---|---|
| Too many candidates | Output grows faster than triage | What share is actually retained? |
| Large change scope | One judgment requires rebuilding too much context | Can the work be split without losing end-to-end meaning? |
| Too much concurrent work | Several state and decision chains wait at once | Has work in progress exceeded coordination capacity? |
| Module coupling | A local change has broad, uncertain effects | Are contracts and impact analysis missing? |
| Duplicate state surfaces | Reviewers cannot identify the currently effective record | Are sources competing? |
| Evidence and Claim mismatch | Lots of material leaves the question unanswered | Is the intended use clear? |
| Growth of governance objects | Review shifts to future proof obligations | Which present danger or delivery Claim is at stake? |
| Too little qualified review | Expertise, time, or authority is insufficient | Do we need a specialist, training, or a different division of work? |
| Document and history debt | Each decision needs background rebuilt | Can we distinguish past and current state? |
| Poor tool interface | Important differences and risks are hard to inspect | Is information missing, or is it hard to find? |

Several causes can coexist. When the problem is work in progress, limiting new work acts directly on it. When modules are coupled, the architecture matters. When evidence misses the claim, more logs may repeat the gap. When a security specialist is missing, an automated summary cannot supply that specialization or authority. Diagnose before choosing a response.

## 11.6 Review Fatigue Can Change the Quality of Decisions

*Review Fatigue* is reduced attention, discrimination, and risk sensitivity under sustained review pressure. Signs can include approving faster while rework and exceptions rise, losing sensitivity to repeated alerts, repeating an agent summary as the decision, losing important constraints in a template, accepting a large change because of sunk cost, offering fewer alternatives or narrower scopes, and turning independent review into a format check.

Simpler tasks, stronger automated evidence, and improved team skill can explain a shorter review. More time spent can also reflect harder work. Consider several observations together.

```text
review latency
decision reversals
escaped incidents
rework
scope corrections
evidence drill-down rate
high-risk finding rate
reviewer disagreement
```

These are candidate observations. *Automation Bias* also needs a concrete description. Reviewers may be unable to tell who produced a summary or which sources it used. Builders and reviewers can share the same wrong premise. A machine pass can be used for a decision outside its grant. Counterevidence, unknowns, and access to originals can disappear. A missing or broken detector can make the alert rate look reassuring.

## 11.7 Cognitive Debt Concerns Future Judgment

*Cognitive Debt* consists of changes already in the system whose rationale, implications, or constraints the team has yet to understand well enough for a foreseeable decision. Compiler output, mature dependencies, and low-risk generated code can be controlled through contracts, tests, versions, and replacement boundaries.

Look for a change that has been accepted or deployed, a foreseeable maintenance, risk, or responsibility decision, and a missing explanation, evidence, or owner. The summary may fail to lead to the source. Important assumptions or limitations may be gone.

### Neighboring Problems

| Problem | What is missing or costly | Typical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Technical Debt | Implementation and architecture choices raise the cost of future change | Fragility, repetition, difficulty extending |
| Cognitive Debt | The team cannot reconstruct a model for a future decision | Misjudgment, difficult takeover, dependence on the original executor |
| State Management Gap | Current state lacks reliable identity, updates, or conflict rules | Incorrect recovery or use of outdated records |
| Evidence Gap | A consequential Claim lacks checkable support for the intended use | Inability to verify or make a bounded disposition |

These problems can reinforce each other and still call for different repairs.

### How Cognitive Debt Builds Up

```text
Change accepted
→ rationale not retained
→ impact and assumptions remain implicit
→ original context disappears
→ next maintainer reconstructs from fragments
→ uncertainty and review cost increase
```

### How a Team Can Pay It Down

Narrow a claim, remove unneeded objects, separate history from current state, link Decisions, Assumptions, and Evidence, restore a repeatable verification path, assign an owner and takeover boundary, refactor tightly coupled code, or downgrade or revoke a capability the team cannot maintain. Documentation pays down debt when it helps with a future judgment. Repeated accounts of the same state can add work.

---

## 11.8 Ownership Erodes When Teams Lose Practical Control

*Ownership Erosion* is the gradual loss of the responsible team's ability to explain, judge, change, and take over the system. A team that has not read every dependency can still exercise ownership when it can do the following.

- Explain the goal and key constraints.
- Locate current State Claims and sources.
- Tell which conclusions evidence supports.
- Find important Decisions and their authority.
- Identify high-risk capabilities.
- Pause, reject, or narrow a change.
- Isolate, roll back, or correct a failure.
- Replace an agent, model, tool, or workflow.
- Maintain the project after the original executor leaves.

A team that can only repeat “the tests passed” or “the agent says it is done,” without locating coverage, runtime identity, risk, and recovery, may have lost practical ownership even while it retains the repository.

```text
Direct implementation inspection
Component and contract review
System behavior and evidence review
Governance and authority review
```

These forms of control can coexist. Safety-critical code can require line-by-line inspection. A low-risk generated asset can rely on sampling and experience review. A long-running delegated workflow can also need governance and a way to take over.

## 11.9 What the Cases Show About Load

The [case record on cognitive load](../../Evidence/receipts/ER-20260824-001__CLM-C11-001.md) preserves observations, contemporaneous judgments, alternatives, and limitations. [Chapter 23](/chapters/23-en.html) gives the sequence. The inspected v4.94 package had `2377` tracked files. D-073 through D-075 added governance and review artifacts without corresponding progress in source, runtime, tests, or qualification. In SP122, a human directly inspected a bounded evidence chain before issuing a disposition.

At the time I judged that review and understanding had not kept up with generation and that I had less control at the level of implementation. That was an experience-based judgment. The file count describes the package.

A bounded inference is that more things to review did not by themselves maintain effective control at that stage. Attention may have moved away from current behavior and delivery evidence toward future obligations to prove things. That is a reason to choose review unit, timing, and target explicitly. File count cannot establish Human Judgment Load. The causes of Governance Livelock and the relative merits of Milestone Review or a Human Control Plane remain open. SP122 also gives counterevidence to a broad move away from direct inspection. Substantive human review was both needed and feasible there.

## 11.10 Diagnose a Mismatch in a Specific Window

### 1. What Is the Decision Window?

- When is the judgment due?
- What would delay cost?
- Can new work pause safely?
- Is this a temporary incident or a persistent operating pattern?

### 2. What Changes Were Retained?

- Which candidates entered the worktree, baseline, release, or runtime?
- Which were only explorations?
- Which were discarded, superseded, or isolated?
- Are many intermediate objects unowned?

### 3. Which Changes Need Human Judgment?

- Which claims can deterministic checks cover?
- Which involve direction, risk acceptance, creativity, responsibility, or authority?
- Which need better automated impact analysis?
- Are all changes being routed to humans indiscriminately?

### 4. How Much Qualified Review Is Available?

- Which expertise is needed?
- Do reviewers have authority for the dispositions?
- Can they access the sources, evidence, and tools they need?
- Can automation expand review capacity in ways that help someone judge?

### 5. What Has Happened So Far?

- Is the Review Backlog growing persistently?
- Do high-risk decisions age or get bypassed?
- Are approvals mechanical? Do rework, scope corrections, or escaped incidents recur?
- Can another person take over after the original executor leaves?
- Can reviewers inspect sources behind the summaries they use?

### 6. Which Alternatives Could Explain It?

- Are work in progress, parallel agents, or change size excessive?
- Does coupling produce overly broad effects?
- Is state duplicated? Are document debt or Governance Overload present?
- Does the evidence answer a different Claim?
- Is the shortage of reviewers temporary?

Bind the answers to a particular period and risk surface before choosing a response.

## 11.11 Reduce Incoming Change

*Work in Progress* (WIP) is the set of work in a given window with open Work or Attempt relationships that continue to consume production or judgment capacity. It is a way of measuring capacity demand. Work and Work Closure keep the meanings they have in Chapter 7.

Limit parallel agents or tasks. Use smaller scopes for high-impact changes. Pause new candidates when review backlog exceeds a threshold. Distinguish exploration from production. Remove duplicate reports, manifests, and gates. Use expiration and cleanup rules for unadopted candidates. Complete an end-to-end slice before expanding.

This can lower visible output and delay exploration. When arrival rate is the cause, limiting it directly changes the workload.

---

## 11.12 Reduce the Unit of Judgment and Its Dependencies

A change can require reconstructing too much context at once. Split independently verifiable vertical slices, define contracts and non-goals, isolate experiments, move historical material out of active views, reduce shared mutable state, trace cross-repository impact, or stage a large migration so each stage can be observed.

Very small diffs can lose end-to-end meaning. Every step can pass while the system stays unusable. Too many slices can also create handoffs, reviews, and governance. Choose a unit that is:

```text
small enough to understand
large enough to carry a meaningful Claim
bounded enough to verify
recoverable enough to stop
```

---

## 11.13 Expand Qualified Review Capacity

Projects can bring in specialists or a second reviewer, provide a reproducible environment, improve diffs, impact analysis, and source links, train the team on the Production Intelligence System, divide work across specialties, use AI to locate differences and propose counterexamples, automate repeatable checks, and keep useful review checklists.

Additional reviewers can add handoffs, disagreements, and waiting. AI reviewers can share a builder's erroneous context. Expansion helps when it adds review capacity or independence the work actually needs.

---

## 11.14 Automate Decisions That Can Be Formalized

Schema validity, build success, out-of-scope diffs, unauthorized inventory changes, regressions, missing contract fields, and artifact revision binding are candidates for automatic verification.

Keep three questions answerable.

1. What did the validator observe?
2. Which Claim and scope does its evidence support?
3. Which decision can use a pass under the applicable grant?

A pass can reduce work on formal checks. Necessity of scope, design choices, user experience, risk acceptance, adoption, release, and promotion each still need their own basis.

## 11.15 Route Direct Review and Compression by Risk

### Prefer Direct Inspection for Consequential Surfaces

- Security and authorization boundaries.
- Irreversible data changes.
- External releases and real effects on users.
- Key architectural contracts.
- The risk of polluting training data, memory, or long-running rules.
- Creative and experience changes involving substantial judgment.
- Detectors, validators, and recovery mechanisms.

### Use Compression, Sampling, or Delegation Where They Fit

- Local, low-risk, reversible changes.
- Repeated actions under mature contracts.
- Mechanical edits covered by strong deterministic checks.
- Isolated experiments that can be rolled back quickly.
- Statistical checks or sampling over large, similar outputs.

Keep these elements visible in a compressed view.

```text
Decision Target
Change Scope
Risk and Impact
Evidence and Limitations
Unknowns and Counterevidence
Authority
Drill-down
Stop / Recovery
```

Compression can save reading and conceal common failure modes. Chapter 12 takes up Progressive Disclosure and the Human Decision Interface. It asks how to maintain source, coverage, and unknowns while presenting a more useful view.

## 11.16 Stop, Rebaseline, or Recover

Stopping may be appropriate when no one owns high-risk decisions, the baseline cannot be identified, evidence cannot be bound to the runtime subject, the change is too large to review, a validator or detector has lost valid operating conditions, the backlog keeps growing, or the team cannot explain isolation or recovery.

Possible responses include temporarily freezing work, narrowing the current Claim, revoking or lowering delegation, returning to an accepted baseline that remains safe, establishing a forward-recovery baseline, isolating a poorly understood capability, and gathering evidence for the decision that actually needs to be made. The goal is to make judgment possible again.

## 11.17 A Cognitive Load Budget Sets Local Limits

A *Cognitive Load Budget* is a set of local constraints on concurrency, open decisions, review delay, and information surfaces for a risk and time window. It can trigger action before the work exceeds capacity.

```yaml
decision_window: release-candidate
scope: save-migration

max_concurrent_high_risk_tasks: 1
max_unresolved_high_risk_decisions: 2
max_review_age_hours: 24

direct_inspection_required_for:
  - irreversible_data_change
  - authority_boundary

machine_verification_allowed_for:
  - schema_conformance
  - bounded_regression

compression_requirements:
  - source_links
  - evidence_scope
  - unknowns
  - recovery_path

over_budget_actions:
  - freeze_new_candidates
  - reduce_scope
  - request_qualified_reviewer
```

This is one design example. Thresholds come from the actual risks, team capacity, and observed history. Interpret them alongside quality and safety measures. Hiding problems, suppressing alerts, or delegating a decision to the wrong system could otherwise appear to lower the load.

## 11.18 Keep People in the Supervision and Intervention Loop

Human work can shift from implementing changes to setting intent and boundaries, deciding what to delegate, maintaining product, architectural, and creative direction, handling exceptions, deciding scope, acceptance, adoption, and release, reviewing the production system, and stopping or taking over a failure.

*Human-on-the-loop* describes the role of supervising and intervening as the work proceeds. For it to provide control, people need access to evidence that fits the decision, identifiable authority and delegation, visible anomalies and unknowns, and the ability to inspect sources, challenge, veto, and take over. Stop and recovery paths need to work. Keep direct review wherever high risk calls for it.

`SYSTEM STABLE` tells a person little about whether they can exercise that control without Observation Coverage, Detector Health, and Source Freshness.

## 11.20 Principles

1. **Observe retained changes that require judgment.** Raw output has its own costs.
2. **Assess the mismatch under named conditions.** AI speed, project scale, and human capacity can change.
3. **Choose the scope of direct inspection.** Consider claims, risk, evidence, and authority.
4. **Use the five perspectives to locate load.** Several can apply at once.
5. **Budget for governance.** Gates and artifacts take attention to use and maintain.
6. **Choose a response after diagnosis.** Limit incoming work, split it, add capacity, verify, route by risk, or recover.
7. **Keep sources reachable through compression.** Shorter text can still omit important conditions.
8. **Bind Cognitive Debt to future decisions.**
9. **Measure ownership in the ability to exercise control.**
10. **Keep conclusions within the scope of the case.** v4.94 leaves general causation and architecture choices open.

## 11.21 Common Anti-Patterns

- Directly equating file, token, or task count with cognitive burden.
- Assuming all generation is retained and needs human reading.
- Holding Human Review Capacity fixed.
- Equating review with reading every line.
- Building a dashboard as the first response to a backlog.
- Adding governance artifacts to address governance overload.
- Judging control by summary length or alert count.
- Treating few alerts as evidence of health.
- Letting an AI reviewer automatically endorse an AI builder.
- Substituting higher-level review for necessary safety-critical implementation review.
- Treating unread code as cognitive debt.
- Using Human-on-the-loop to obscure a loss of human authority.
- Generalizing a single v4.94 case to all AI-native projects.

## 11.22 Checklist

### Establish the Mismatch

- What is the decision window?
- How much output was actually retained?
- What still needs human judgment?
- Which expertise and authority do those judgments require?
- Is the backlog temporary or persistent?
- What rework, missed findings, scope corrections, or takeover problems have appeared?
- Have we considered WIP, coupling, duplicate state, governance growth, and a shortage of reviewers?

### Choose the Response

- Can we pause or slow new candidates?
- Can the change unit shrink while keeping a meaningful end-to-end Claim?
- Can we add genuinely independent, qualified review?
- Which judgments can deterministic verification handle?
- Which targets need direct inspection?
- Does compression preserve source, scope, unknowns, and drill-down?
- Is the baseline clear, safe to use, and recoverable?
- Did we count the cost of governance?

### Prevent Ownership Erosion

- Can the team explain current goals and key constraints?
- Can it locate State Claims, Evidence, and Decisions?
- Can it distinguish a test pass from acceptance, adoption, and release?
- Can it pause, veto, downgrade, or take over the work?
- Can another qualified role continue after the original agent or team member leaves?
- Do consequential surfaces still get the direct review they require?

---

## 11.23 Conclusion

Measure whether retained changes that need human judgment persistently exceed qualified review capacity within a particular decision window. If they do, choose a response that acts on the cause.

```text
reduce incoming work
reduce scope and coupling
increase qualified review capacity
automate claim-specific verification
route review by risk
compress with drill-down
stop, rebaseline or recover
```

Several responses can work together. Each has a cost. Direct human attention belongs where judgment, responsibility, and experience matter most. Keep the choice visible, verifiable, and reversible. The next chapter asks how a system can compress information while preserving uncertainty, counterevidence, and blind spots.
