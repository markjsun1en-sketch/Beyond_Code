# Chapter 13. Governed Autonomy
## Delegation is a configuration a project can withdraw

This chapter proposes a model that distinguishes Capability, Permission, Authority Grant, Reliance Disposition, Autonomy Profile, and Responsibility. The AI Game Dev OS v4.94 case supports boundaries within that project. It leaves the model's cross-project suitability and any effects on efficiency or governance cost open.

[Chapter 12](/chapters/12-en.html) describes the Human Decision Interface. A person can inspect decisions, evidence, unknowns, authority, and available actions there. The authority behind each action comes from elsewhere.

That leaves several questions.

- What can the agent do?
- What does the platform let it do?
- What has the organization authorized it to do?
- Which actions can proceed without individual approval?
- What conditions trigger a pause, more supervision, or takeover?
- How should operational history affect a decision to rely on the agent?
- Who can expand or revoke delegation?

An "autonomy level" obscures the answers. A more capable agent can have the same grant as before. A project can also choose close human involvement when that is the best fit for its work.

**Governed Autonomy** configures the actions a system can take without individual human approval under named authority, scope, policy, evidence, supervision, and recovery conditions. The configuration can be versioned, can expire, and can be paused, narrowed, or revoked.

---

## 13.1 Automation can differ from one action to the next

A system may:

- Read a repository automatically and require approval to write.
- Modify an isolated worktree automatically and lack authority to commit.
- Commit automatically and require a separate grant to merge.
- Merge into a development branch automatically and leave release to a different role.
- Run tests automatically and have no standing to accept implementation scope.
- Accept one narrow criterion automatically and leave adoption open.
- Act automatically in a low-risk environment and advise only in production.

The original outline used the following labels.

~~~text
L0 Advisory
L1 Generate, Human executes
L2 Execute, Human approves each time
L3 Low risk auto, high risk approval
L4 Goal-level autonomy with monitoring
L5 Highly autonomous, exception only
~~~

They can describe operating modes in a particular project. A single project may use several at once.

~~~text
code suggestion          advisory
isolated test execution  bounded_auto
repository write         approval_required
development commit       auto_with_audit
production deployment    human_or_release_gate
policy modification      prohibited
~~~

A higher share of automated work can widen the surface of unsupervised failure if observation, recovery, or authority is weak. Removing a checkpoint needs to fit the conditions of that action.

## 13.2 Six objects in the model

### Capability

**Capability** concerns what the system can do technically. A model and its tools might be able to edit files, run commands, call an external API, or generate a deployment artifact. Permission and authority have their own sources.

### Permission

**Permission** describes what the execution environment exposes in practice. The sandbox, tools, credentials, and platform determine the available operations. Whether the organization authorizes one of those operations for a given purpose is a separate question.

### Authority Grant

An **Authority Grant** records an authorized source permitting a named subject to take or decide a class of action for a specified target. It answers who grants what action, to whom, and for which scope, conditions, and use.

The grant carries the permission in the organizational sense. A Decision or Policy Record contains what an authorized subject decided under a valid grant. A Disposition records the outcome for a named Decision Target. Successful runs, technical Permission, an Autonomy Profile, and a Decision Record can refer to the grant. The grantor remains the source of authority to create or expand it.

### Reliance Disposition

A **Reliance Disposition** is the outcome of a Decision Record about a named use of evidence. It answers whether the current evidence is sufficient for the project to rely on something for that use.

Bind the Decision Target to a capability, configuration, environment, time, and intended use. A disposition can be provisional, restricted, or accepted for scope. Authority to act is still a separate grant.

### Autonomy Profile

An **Autonomy Profile** is a versioned configuration that puts existing authority into effect through runtime supervision, approval, escalation, audit, and recovery. It specifies when an already authorized action can proceed without individual approval. The underlying delegation comes from its grant.

### Responsibility

**Responsibility** is an assigned duty to do, maintain, respond to, or recover a piece of work. Someone must execute, maintain, review, escalate, or restore an automation boundary.

Agents can be assigned operational responsibility. Accountability and authority must be established separately. [Chapter 18](/chapters/18-en.html) defines Accountability as an organizational relationship in which a named person or role is answerable for explanation, review, correction, or consequences. Legal liability depends on jurisdiction, institutions, and case facts.

These objects constrain the same work.

~~~text
Capability            technical possibility
Permission            executable surface
Authority Grant       normative authorization for named use
Autonomy Profile      runtime delegation conditions
        │
        └── together delimit the eligible action surface
                         │
                         ▼
                      Execution
                         │ produces observations and evidence
                         ▼
                 scoped Reliance Claim
                         │ informs an Authority-bound Decision Record
                         ▼
              scoped Reliance Disposition

Responsibility
cross-cuts the work chain

Accountability
is assigned by the organizational control model
~~~

Execution produces evidence that can support or challenge a scoped Reliance Claim. A subject holding the relevant grant can use that evidence to make a new Decision Record. The evidence itself leaves the Reliance Disposition, Permission, Authority Grant, and Autonomy Profile unchanged until the relevant authorized action occurs.

## 13.3 The dimensions of an Autonomy Profile

A field such as `level: 4` leaves most of the operating conditions unspecified. This example is one possible schema.

~~~yaml
profile_id: AUT-PROFILE-042
version: 3
status: active

subject:
  agent_role: implementation-agent
  agent_contract_version: 7
  model_adapter: model-profile-a
  workflow_version: wf-implementation-12

authority_ref: AUTH-GRANT-108
capability: modify_gameplay_source
action_classes:
  - read
  - write
  - run_bounded_tests
  - create_development_commit

scope:
  repository: example-game
  paths:
    - src/gameplay/**
    - tests/gameplay/**
  environment: isolated-worktree
  excluded:
    - release/**
    - production-data
    - policy/**

supervision:
  write: bounded_auto
  test_execution: bounded_auto
  commit: auto_with_audit
  merge: approval_required
  deploy: prohibited

preconditions:
  - clean_bound_worktree
  - intent_scope_resolved
  - required_test_harness_available

evidence_requirements:
  - diff_scope
  - focused_tests
  - unresolved_assumptions

escalation_triggers:
  - scope_growth
  - external_dependency_change
  - authority_boundary_contact
  - target_non_result

protected_stop_conditions:
  - write_outside_scope
  - credential_request
  - production_state_contact

recovery:
  checkpoint_required: true
  rollback_ref: RECOVERY-021
  forward_recovery_allowed: true

expires_at: 2026-09-30
revocation_authority_ref: AUTH-REVOKE-004
~~~

An Autonomy Profile should identify:

- Subject configuration.
- Capability and action classes.
- Repository, path, environment, and data scope.
- Authority reference.
- Supervision mode.
- Preconditions.
- Evidence requirements.
- Escalation triggers.
- Protected stop conditions.
- Recovery.
- Expiry and revocation.

A change in model, contract, workflow, tool permissions, or environment can change the identity of the delegated subject, even when an agent keeps the same name.

## 13.4 Recheck boundaries when a capability changes

An agent may have a solid record of implementing gameplay. Changes to governance, authority policy, deployment, memory promotion, the skill registry, security boundaries, and production data involve other capabilities. The old record covers the configuration, capability, and intended use it actually observed.

For a new capability, check:

~~~text
subject identity
action surface
scope
impact
irreversibility
observation
verification
recovery
authority source
~~~

A low-risk operation within the same contract may already be covered. Use a boundary as fine-grained as the risk calls for. A new resource, environment, Decision Target, or class of irreversible action may need an explicit extension or a new grant. Similar descriptions or a familiar agent identity are insufficient to establish that an old grant applies.

---

## 13.5 Tie automation defaults to the risk surface

The presence of a capability leaves the default permission open. A project policy could allow read-only search, pure computation in an isolated environment, and checks without side effects. Explicit grants or deny-by-default policies fit actions such as:

- Writing to a protected source.
- Changing authority, permission, or policy.
- Accessing credentials.
- Writing to external systems.
- Committing, merging, releasing, or deploying.
- Modifying production data.
- Running an irreversible migration.
- Promoting memory, skills, or workflows.
- Expanding one's own tools or runtime boundary.

Set the defaults against these dimensions.

~~~text
action class
resource class
environment
impact
reversibility
existing organizational policy
~~~

Each default should protect against an identified risk. For actions already covered by policy, a forced manual click may serve no purpose.

## 13.6 Align technical Permission with Authority

Common Permissions include:

~~~text
Read
Write
Execute
Commit
Merge
Deploy
External Read
External Write
Credential Use
Memory Update
Skill Update
Workflow Update
Policy Update
Permission Change
~~~

A list alone does not establish authority. A Git credential may technically allow a push. The task may authorize a local commit only, and release policy may call for an independent gate. A tool misconfiguration can still make the push possible.

~~~text
Technical Permission > Granted Authority
~~~

Keep technical Permission within current Authority where possible. The Authority record also needs a Decision Target, subject, resource and environment, time, preconditions, delegation depth, redelegation terms, audit and recovery provisions, and revocation. Enforcement guards the technical boundary. The Authority record explains why the action is allowed.

## 13.7 Configure autonomy around risk conditions

A single Risk Score need not carry all the conditions that affect autonomy. Inspect the dimensions that matter to the proposed action.

~~~text
Granted Action Surface
Impact
Irreversibility
State Uncertainty
Verification Difficulty
Observation Coverage
Recovery Cost
External Exposure
Decision Authority
~~~

One capable model might use `bounded_auto` in a read-only, isolated, replayable environment. For a production data migration it may propose a plan only. A narrow authorized gate may accept a specific criterion automatically while adoption and release stay with their assigned roles.

When conditions change, the response can vary.

~~~text
continue within scope
narrow scope
collect evidence
isolate
switch supervision mode
invoke delegated gate
pause
block
escalate
~~~

Choose based on the current risk, the role involved, and the recovery options.

## 13.8 Structural Assurance and Operational Evidence inform reliance

The original material distinguished `Assured Trust` from `Earned Trust`. The underlying questions can be expressed in terms of design controls and observed operation.

### Structural Assurance

**Structural Assurance** consists of controls supplied in advance by boundaries, isolation, verification, and recovery design. Examples include:

- Bounded authority.
- Sandboxing.
- Deterministic checks.
- Versioned baselines.
- Stop and recovery paths.
- Independent review.
- Observation Coverage.

A new capability can have some Structural Assurance before its first observed run.

### Operational Evidence

**Operational Evidence** is inspectable observation from a named configuration in particular environments and tasks. It can include:

- Runs.
- Failures and rework.
- Rollback exercises.
- Human interventions.
- QA reopens.
- Escaped defects.
- Audits and incidents.

Operational history can support or challenge a Reliance Claim.

~~~text
Structural Assurance
+ Operational Evidence
→ supports or challenges a scoped Reliance Claim
→ informs an Authority-bound Decision Record
→ produces a scoped Reliance Disposition
~~~

A tempting inference is:

~~~text
success history
→ earned Authority
~~~

Authority still comes from authorized grantors and policy. Even after stable operation, legal requirements, product responsibility, or separation of duties can call for a human or an independent gate. A new capability can receive limited delegation within a narrow, reversible and well-isolated scope before it has an operating history.

## 13.9 Record scoped reliance

A number such as `Trust Score = 87.3` hides questions about the capability, configuration, environment, task type, time window, intended use, evidence, and authority.

A scoped Reliance Disposition can retain those answers.

~~~yaml
reliance_id: REL-042
subject_ref: AUT-PROFILE-042
capability: modify_gameplay_source
environment: isolated-worktree
intended_use: development_candidate_generation

disposition: accepted_for_scope
supporting_evidence_refs:
  - EV-RUNS-012
  - EV-ROLLBACK-004
limitations:
  - not qualified for release
  - external dependencies require review
expires_at: 2026-09-30
decision_authority_ref: AUTH-REL-003
~~~

One profile can be accepted for one use and remain `unsupported` or `restricted` for another. Trend measures can help detect change. Keep the scope and decision alongside them.

## 13.10 Authority Grants and Delegation Contracts

**Delegation** occurs when an authorized role assigns a named action or decision to another subject under bounded conditions. The relationship needs an inspectable record. An Authority Grant might read:

~~~yaml
grant_id: AUTH-GRANT-108
grantor: engineering-lead
grantor_authority_ref: AUTH-ORG-001

subject_ref: AUT-PROFILE-042
action_classes:
  - write_gameplay_source
  - create_development_commit
decision_targets:
  - development_candidate

scope:
  repository: example-game
  paths:
    - src/gameplay/**
  environment: isolated-worktree

preconditions:
  - intent_scope_resolved
  - no_production_credentials

prohibited:
  - merge
  - deploy
  - policy_change
  - downstream_delegation

evidence_obligations:
  - scoped_diff
  - focused_tests
  - unresolved_assumptions

valid_from: 2026-08-24
expires_at: 2026-09-30
revocation_ref: AUTH-REVOKE-004
~~~

The grantor needs authority to grant the actions. If **Downstream Delegation** is allowed, a delegated subject can pass on some actions. Specify:

- Maximum delegation depth.
- Action classes eligible for redelegation.
- Subject eligibility.
- Whether scope carries forward or narrows.
- Evidence and audit duties.
- How revocation cascades.

The ability to create a subagent, on its own, tells us nothing about whether an existing grant can be transferred.

## 13.11 Assign Supervision Modes by action

| Mode | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `advisory` | Recommend without making the actual change | Architecture candidates or product direction |
| `human_executes` | Prepare steps for a person to execute | A high-risk one-off operation |
| `approval_required` | Prepare the action and get each invocation approved by an authorized role | Data migration or permission changes |
| `bounded_auto` | Execute automatically within fixed scope and preconditions | Isolated tests or local changes |
| `auto_with_audit` | Execute automatically with sampling or periodic audit | Established recurring low-risk work |
| `exception_routed` | Automate the normal path and escalate named exceptions | A long-running workflow with a healthy detector |
| `prohibited` | The current configuration forbids the action | Self-expansion of authority or an unauthorized release |

One profile can assign different modes to different actions. The same action may use another mode in a different environment. An exception-routed mode needs coverage, healthy detectors, and recoverability. Without those conditions, work can continue simply because no failure was visible.

## 13.12 Account for the cost of governance

Governance consumes runtime, review time, human attention, state management, proof artifacts, coordination, recovery work, and effort spent on false positives. Additional gates, roles, manifests, and reviews can add cost while leaving an important danger open.

~~~text
Which risk is protected?
At which action boundary?
By which control?
With what detection?
At what cost?
What happens on failure?
~~~

A finding that identifies no reachable unsafe action, possible false promotion, affected authority boundary, or evidence required at this stage may be a Process Warning. Match the strength of the response to the claim, risk, stage, and recovery conditions.

---

## 13.13 Set a Protected Control Floor for actions and environments

A **Protected Control Floor** is the minimum set of controls a subject cannot bypass for an action under current policy. It can include:

- Prohibiting out-of-scope writes.
- Prohibiting access to credentials or production state.
- Prohibiting the use of development evidence as qualification.
- Prohibiting unauthorized merge, release, or policy change.
- Requiring explicit authority and recovery for irreversible actions.
- Requiring important identity and provenance records to be inspectable.

The floor applies to action classes and environments. A project can allow a subject to add review automatically when risk rises.

~~~text
bounded_auto
→ approval_required
→ paused
~~~

Changes to the subject's own grant, control floor, or revocation terms must follow authorized paths. Policy may also allow **de-escalation**, a reduction in supervision or controls. State what evidence allows a mode to resume, who or what gate can decide, which controls fall outside that delegation, and when to reassess. Changes in governance strength come from existing policy or an authorized decision. Successful execution and self-assessment can supply evidence for a decision.

## 13.14 Distinguish a Hard Stop from a Process Warning

A **Hard Stop** immediately halts or isolates work to prevent a reachable high-impact danger, unauthorized action, or invalid promotion. Triggers can include:

- Conflicting Intent or Authority that leaves no authorized interpretation of the action.
- A write, execution, or external access outside scope.
- An irreversible action without the required approval or recovery.
- A loss of Validator Integrity that prevents safe interpretation of the target result.
- Development evidence about to be promoted to qualification or release.
- A subject attempting to expand its own permission, authority, or delegation.
- An unknown baseline identity when proceeding would change protected state.
- Tool behavior touching credentials, production data, or protected external systems.

A **Process Warning** can flag:

- Repeated meta-only successors.
- Growing proof artifacts without progress in source or tests.
- Steadily increasing counts of objects, digests, roles, or gates.
- A review finding that identifies no currently unsafe action.
- More tests with no expansion in the current contract.
- A growing scope, dependency set, or review backlog.

A warning can lead to:

~~~text
narrow claim
reduce scope
re-stage work
collect evidence
request human checkpoint
freeze new successors
~~~

Automatically opening another governance transaction may compound the original problem.

## 13.15 What human participation is for

Human participation can cover:

- Value and product direction.
- Acceptance of high-impact risk.
- Authority grants and revocation.
- Scope, adoption, and release.
- Complex exceptions and conflicts.
- Creative and experience judgments.
- Review of the automation regime itself.

Low-risk, reversible actions can proceed under explicit delegation without approval at each step. **Human Cognitive Authority** preserves final human judgment where the organization reserves it. The organization needs to be able to:

- Locate primary evidence.
- Understand claims, scope, and unknowns.
- Recognize overreach and evidence gaps.
- Change delegation and policy.
- Pause, veto, take over, and recover.
- Bind final responsibility to named roles.

Reserved decisions belong to named human grants or institutional boundaries. The phrase "reserved human decision" can describe them without a separate `Human Decision Right` object. Security, compliance, and separation of duties can require multiple roles or an independent gate. Automated gates can make dispositions for explicitly delegated targets. [Chapter 12](/chapters/12-en.html) describes how to expose the paths in an interface. This chapter specifies where they get authority.

## 13.16 Pause, Restrict, Revoke, Expire, and Takeover

### Pause

Block new actions temporarily and retain the existing profile and grant while evidence or a decision is pending.

### Restrict

Narrow the action, scope, environment, or supervision mode.

### Revoke

An authorized role ends a grant, removing authority for subsequent actions.

### Expire

A grant or profile lapses at a specified time or version, or on a specified condition.

### Takeover

A new authorized subject assumes control of the current task and decides whether to continue, recover, or end it. Before taking over, establish:

- The current candidate and baseline.
- Which actions are complete, in flight, or pending.
- The worktree, locks, tool sessions, and external side effects.
- Current authority and credentials.
- Evidence already produced and outstanding unknowns.
- Whether the stop has actually taken effect.
- Available recovery.
- The incoming subject's grant.

The UI can display the new owner once control has transferred. An irreversible external effect can leave forward recovery as the viable route.

---

## 13.17 Revocation covers in-flight work and downstream grants

Revoking a grant prevents future authorized use. Physical actions already taken have to be reconciled separately.

~~~text
new action denial
in-flight action handling
credential invalidation
subagent / downstream grant cascade
queued task cancellation
external side-effect reconciliation
evidence preservation
recovery ownership
resume criteria
~~~

Record separately whether the authority was revoked, permission closed, execution stopped, external state restored, evidence preserved, and work reassigned. Each can become true at a different time. For high-impact capabilities, exercise pause, credential revocation, takeover, and recovery to see whether the paths work in practice.

## 13.18 Give automated gates a named Decision Target

[Chapter 9](/chapters/09-en.html) distinguishes:

~~~text
Evidence / Validation
Acceptance
Adoption
Release
~~~

A project might grant a gate this decision.

> Accept AC-07 automatically when the named test suite passes against the bound build and environment.

That grant covers AC-07. Necessity of implementation scope, adoption, release, changes to the gate's own tests or thresholds, and expanded delegation each require their own authority.

A delegated gate needs a Decision Target, claim scope, subject or build identity, required evidence, authority source, failure and `non-result` semantics, expiry, override or appeal, and audit. Its output is an authorized disposition within the grant.

## 13.20 Start with one capability

Choose a repeated, costly capability with a clear boundary.

~~~text
modify gameplay source in isolated worktree
run bounded regression suite
create development commit
triage non-production incident
generate release candidate evidence
~~~

1. Identify the subject configuration.
2. Define the capability and action class.
3. Align Permission and Authority.
4. State scope, environment, and exclusions.
5. Pick each action's Supervision Mode.
6. Declare preconditions, evidence, and unknowns.
7. Configure escalation and Protected Stops.
8. Exercise pause, revocation, takeover, and recovery.
9. Set expiry.
10. Collect Operational Evidence.
11. Have an authorized role update reliance or the profile as warranted.

Expand to nearby actions after the project can observe, stop, and recover the operation in real use. Project-wide levels, Trust Scores, and dashboards can wait until an actual decision calls for them.

## 13.22 Principles

1. **Configure autonomy for a subject.** Bind it to capability, scope, environment, and version.
2. **Check Capability, Permission, and Authority separately.** Technical reachability and organizational authorization answer different questions.
3. **Use reliance evidence for reliance decisions.** Structural Assurance and Operational Evidence support or challenge the named claim. An authorized subject makes the disposition for its target.
4. **Use profiles to implement delegation.** Write grant changes through authorized sources.
5. **Choose supervision by action.** One system may need several modes at once.
6. **Tie defaults to risk.** Check the action, environment, and recovery conditions.
7. **Count the cost of governance.** Gates, review, and proof artifacts consume resources.
8. **Set the control floor for an action.** The book proposes no universal G0–G4 progression.
9. **Tie Hard Stops to present danger.** Route a Process Warning according to the issue it names.
10. **Give human participation a purpose.** Make delegation, judgment, and takeover checkable.
11. **Track what revocation does.** Follow authority, permission, execution, external effects, and recovery separately.
12. **Limit gate authority to its target.** Adoption and release require their own grants.

## 13.23 Common failure patterns

- Treating L0–L5 as a maturity path for every project.
- Giving a more capable model more authority by default.
- Upgrading autonomy on a count of past successes.
- Combining capability, permission, authority, and responsibility in one field.
- Relying on a global Trust Score.
- Letting a new capability silently inherit an earlier profile.
- Transferring the full grant automatically when an agent creates a subagent.
- Exposing more platform permissions than the grant covers without enforcement or detection.
- Requiring clicks for every automated action.
- Automatically executing all actions described as low-risk regardless of environment and recovery.
- Adding gates to resolve governance livelock.
- Opening a new governance transaction for every Process Warning.
- Letting an agent reduce its own Protected Control Floor.
- Treating a pause button as proof the work has stopped.
- Revoking a grant while leaving credentials, queues, subagents, or external effects unresolved.
- Using a test pass as a scope acceptance, adoption, or release.
- Letting any person override every policy.
- Generalizing from v4.94 to a universal autonomy schema.

## 13.24 Checklist

### Subject and action

- Which agent, model, contract, workflow, and tool versions identify the subject?
- What is the capability and action class?
- Are environment, resource, data, and path scope clear?
- Has a new capability inherited authority without review?

### Permission and Authority

- Does technical Permission exceed the grant?
- Can the grantor grant these actions?
- Are Decision Target, preconditions, expiry, and prohibitions recorded?
- Is downstream delegation permitted, and how does revocation cascade?

### Autonomy Profile

- Does each action have its own Supervision Mode?
- Do evidence requirements, escalation, and Protected Stops bind the profile?
- Do coverage and Detector Health support `exception_routed` operation?
- Can the profile be paused, narrowed, revoked, or expired?

### Reliance

- Are Structural Assurance and Operational Evidence distinct?
- Does reliance identify capability, configuration, environment, and intended use?
- Is a reliance disposition or test pass being used as a grant?
- Does operational history include failure, rework, rollback, and human intervention along with success?

### Stop and recovery

- Does a Hard Stop identify current danger, overreach, or invalid promotion?
- Could a Process Warning create more governance overload?
- Do new actions actually stop after pause?
- Does revocation handle credentials, queues, subagents, and in-flight actions?
- Does takeover provide the baseline, evidence, unknowns, and recovery?
- Who owns forward recovery for irreversible external effects?

### People and gates

- Which decisions need a person, and which can a delegated gate make?
- Does the Human Decision Interface show the grant behind each control?
- Are acceptance, adoption, and release distinct?
- Does separation of duties permit a single role to override every control?

## 13.25 Conclusion

Governed Autonomy makes delegation inspectable.

~~~text
Capability
+ Permission
+ Authority Grant
+ scoped Reliance
+ Autonomy Profile
+ Observation and Evidence
+ Stop / Revocation / Recovery
~~~

Operational history supports or challenges reliance claims. An authorized subject can use it to make a new Decision Record and scoped Reliance Disposition. Changes in risk and evidence can also trigger a new Supervision Mode under the applicable policy. The authority itself comes from a named grantor within organizational and policy boundaries. A delegated gate exercises a narrow grant.

> **Autonomy does not earn Authority. Autonomy operationalizes Authority that has already been granted for a named scope.**

The next chapter examines the observations this configuration depends on: what happened, which surfaces went unobserved, whether detectors are healthy, and when an intervention is warranted.
